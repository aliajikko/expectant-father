    Learn

    Lessons and the library

    The book's central idea: you run about a trimester behind her. She feels pregnant from the start. For you it becomes real in steps, the heartbeat, the scan, the first kick, and for many men only on meeting the baby. Nearly every feeling on this list is normal, and saying it out loud works better than hiding it.

    What to do with the feelings

    • Say them. Hiding worries to protect her backfires and raises stress for both of you.
    • Fifteen minutes a day about anything except the baby. Most couples see communication drop in the first year after a birth, and many never recover it.
    • Talk to other fathers. The book comes back to this in almost every chapter.
    • Take some time for yourself, and tell her where you are going.
    • When she needs reassurance, "everything will be fine" is the wrong answer. Tell her what is on your mind and that you are still here.

    Five things that predict a more involved father

    Correlational, but the advice holds.

      Sex across the pregnancy

      • Desire can rise or fall, in either of you. Both are normal.
      • In a normal pregnancy sex is safe throughout. The baby is cushioned by fluid. Ask the doctor first if there is any risk of early labour or a low-lying placenta.
      • Each of you tends to underestimate how attractive the other finds you. Talk about it. Offer affection without pressure. Never criticise her body, even as a joke.
      • Late in pregnancy desire usually drops, mostly from discomfort and worry.

      Morning sickness, what you can do

      Affects most women. Usually starts 4 to 6 weeks after conception and eases by 14 to 15 weeks, roughly late October to mid December for this pregnancy.

      • Keep her drinking. A water bottle by the bed.
      • Small meals every 2 to 3 hours, before the nausea starts. Low blood sugar makes it worse. Crackers by the bed for the morning.
      • Keep strong smells away, especially fatty or spicy cooking.
      • Take over the cooking and the shopping. Learn a few 10 to 30 minute meals.
      • Ginger, vitamin B6 and acupressure wrist bands help some women. Clear each with her doctor first.
      • If the prenatal vitamins make it worse, ask about taking them with food or switching type.
      • Don't nag about food. Eat as well as she does, in solidarity.
      • No nausea is not a warning sign. Nausea that has not eased by 14 to 15 weeks, or weight loss, means call the doctor.

      The rules for the first trimester

      • Folic acid daily. The main window for organ development runs to about mid November.
      • No alcohol, smoking, vaping or recreational drugs. Caffeine kept low.
      • No medicine, prescription or over the counter, without checking with her doctor.
      • Nothing that pushes her temperature above 39°C, especially in the first 6 to 8 weeks. Worth remembering in this climate.
      • Keep exercising if her doctor agrees. Swimming, walking and snorkelling are fine. No scuba, no contact sports, nothing with a risk of falling.
      • Don't panic about anything she did before she knew.
      • Your side: stop smoking if you smoke, and take over cat litter, garden chemicals and pesticides.
      The book gives her vitamin D target as 600 mcg. That is a unit error. The usual figure is 600 IU, about 15 mcg. Take the doctor's numbers, not the book's.

      At the appointments

      • Go to as many as you can. It makes the pregnancy real, shows her she is not doing this alone, and staff take you more seriously.
      • Tell the doctor from the start that you want to be involved, and ask questions at every visit.
      • Choosing the doctor and place of birth: ask about their attitude to fathers in the room, their view on C-sections and induction, who covers when they are away, and the after-hours line. She gets at least 51 percent of the vote.
      • Decide before results arrive what you would want to know from screening. If she is 35 or over, more screening is usually offered. That is routine.
      • Visits are usually monthly to about month 6, then every two weeks, then weekly in the last month.

      Ways to show her you care

      • Unprompted chores. Back rubs and foot rubs.
      • Learn a few recipes she likes.
      • Never comment on her weight.
      • Go to every appointment you can, and say in advance that you are coming.
      • Buy something for the baby together.
      • Take a belly photo every month.
      • Set ground rules with relatives about advice and visits, and be the one who enforces them.

      If something goes wrong

      • Miscarriage is common, a fifth to a third of pregnancies, mostly in the first three months. More than nine in ten couples who have one go on to a healthy baby.
      • Your grief is real. Men often grieve quietly and nobody asks how they are.
      • What helps her: take the initiative, listen, don't try to fix it. Never say "we can always have another one".
      • Bleeding, or sharp one-sided pain low in the abdomen, needs to be seen the same day.
      What she eats is the one part of the pregnancy you can take over almost entirely. The book's nutrition chapter, in reads, with the American product advice left out. Which local fish are safe is still a question for her doctor.

      Before it starts

      • Agree her stance on pain relief well ahead, and a code word that means "I really want the drugs now". She gets the 51 percent. She should not be making big decisions mid-labour.
      • Decide your part in advance: cutting the cord, holding the baby first, what happens if there is a C-section.
      • Keep the birth plan short and flexible. Ask the doctor whether they want it written.
      • Bags packed and transport sorted by month 8. The bag list is under Tools.
      • Call the doctor before you leave for the hospital. Their instruction outranks her wish to stay home longer.

      If it is a C-section

      • Ask early what the hospital allows for fathers in theatre, and about skin-to-skin contact.
      • Sit at her head. Fathers do sometimes faint. Tell her what is happening with the baby.
      • Go with the baby if they take it to the nursery, and offer skin-to-skin on your chest.
      • Afterwards, tell her she did not fail. Many women feel a C-section as failure, and unchecked that can feed postpartum depression.
      • Never suggest a C-section yourself. Let the doctor raise it.
      • Recovery takes weeks: no lifting, no stairs, no driving for a while. Covering that is your job.
      • Fear that she will die is one of fathers' commonest fears. A frightening birth can leave you with post-traumatic stress, and men rarely ask for help. Ask.

      If the baby comes before you can get there

      A bigger risk on an island than in the book's settings. Follow the emergency operator over anything here.

      1. Call emergency services first.
      2. Keep her sheltered, with a pillow under her hips.
      3. Don't pull the baby's head. Support it. If the cord is around the neck, slide it gently over the head. Clear mucus from the mouth and nose.
      4. Support the body as it comes. Don't slap the baby.
      5. Baby on her chest, skin to skin, and let it nurse if it will. That helps the uterus contract. Dry and cover them both.
      6. Tie the cord 8 to 10 cm from the baby. Don't cut it unless the operator says to.
      7. Don't pull on the cord for the placenta. It comes 5 to 30 minutes later. Keep it in a bag for the doctor.
      8. Massage her lower belly after the placenta to limit bleeding.

      Kit: waterproof pads or newspaper, non-latex gloves, a suction bulb, a cord clamp or clean string, a sealable bag, clean scissors, towels.

      Her recovery

      • Bleeding goes from red to pink to brown to yellow over about six weeks.
      • Soreness from stitches or a C-section wound fades over about six weeks.
      • Breasts swell and harden from about day 3, nipples sore for about two weeks.
      • Exhaustion, constipation, sweating, hair loss for some months, mixed feelings, and grief at no longer being the centre of attention are all common.
      • Sex: doctors usually say wait 4 to 6 weeks. Low desire after that is mostly tiredness, not her body.

      Her mood: blues, depression, psychosis

      • Take the night shifts so she gets at least five hours of unbroken sleep.
      • Tell her it is not her fault.
      • Keep a quiet note of her mood day to day, so you can tell blues from depression. The Log screen does this.

      Safe sleep

      • On the back, every time.
      • A firm, flat mattress and nothing else in the cot. No pillows, bumpers or loose bedding.
      • In your room, not your bed, for at least the first six months.
      • No smoking anywhere near the baby. No talc.
      • Dress the baby like you, plus a hat. Don't overdress.

      You

      • You will probably feel pushed out at some point. Nearly every father in one large study did. Build your own relationship through everything that isn't feeding: bathing, changing, carrying, walking. Start in hospital.
      • The bond may not be instant. A quarter to 40 percent of parents feel indifferent at first. Most fathers bond within two months, through hands-on care.
      • Your hormones change too. Testosterone drops and prolactin rises, which makes you more responsive to crying.
      • Jealousy of the baby or of her is common and does the most damage when buried. Tell her or a friend.
      • Fathers get postpartum depression too, roughly 1 in 10. In men it looks like anger, irritability, withdrawal, working longer hours, exhaustion, indecision. It usually starts a week or two after the birth. Most people spot it in a mother and miss it in a father. Get help early. The weekly check-in under Log watches for it.

      Crying and anger

      • Respond every time. You can't spoil a newborn. Most cry 20 minutes to an hour a day, peaking at 6 to 8 weeks.
      • Take turns in 20 to 30 minute shifts.
      • If you feel rage rising, put the baby down safely in the cot and leave the room. Come back when you are calm. Everyone has dark thoughts at 3 am. Acting on them is what matters.
      • Never shake a baby. Never throw one in the air.
      • Call the baby's doctor if crying goes on for more than two hours.

      Running the house

      • You handle cooking, shopping, laundry, calls and visitors.
      • Short visits, few at a time, and everyone washes their hands for the first month.
      • Keep a list of jobs to hand to anyone who asks "can I help?"
      • Support breastfeeding with praise, food and water. Feeds run 8 to 12 times in 24 hours, more than the book says.
      • Relatives who stay may take over. The book says the two of you decide. Where family help is the norm and welcome, adapt that rather than apply it.

      Kick counter

      From about month 5 she will feel the baby move. A common way to count is to time how long it takes to feel ten movements, at a time of day when the baby is usually active. Tap once per kick, roll or jab. A clear change from the baby's usual pattern, especially much less movement, is a reason to call the doctor the same day. The book has no counting rule of its own, so this is the general method.
